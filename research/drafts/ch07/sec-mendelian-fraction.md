## How much of inherited disease runs through splicing {#sec-mendelian-fraction}

### The headline fractions and where they came from

Several figures circulate for the splicing share of inherited disease, and they do not converge. A machine-learning estimate over exonic variants put the splice-disrupting fraction at approximately 16 per cent of inherited disease exonic mutations, and approximately 10 to 14 per cent of cancer exonic mutations [@mort2014mutpredsplice]. That is a predicted share rather than an assayed one, and it covers exonic variants only. Those authors add that their cancer set contains a large proportion of passenger variants, which they say will almost certainly make the cancer figure a serious under-estimate of the splicing-sensitive share of cancer driver mutations. The direction of that error is stated, which is more than most of these figures offer. A massively parallel splicing assay of 4,964 exonic disease alleles drawn from HGMD found about 10 per cent of them, 513 variants, disrupting splicing both in cells and in vitro, against 3 per cent of the common SNPs tested the same way [@soemedi2017mapsy]. The SpliceAI authors instead estimated that cryptic splicing causes 9 to 11 per cent of the pathogenic mutations carried by patients who have rare genetic disorders [@jaganathan2019spliceai]. One of those three is a prediction over exonic variants, one a reporter measurement, one a prediction over patient mutations.

The oldest figure has no measurement behind it. One third of disease alleles altering splicing enters this literature as the assay paper's summary of previous studies, not as a result that paper reports [@soemedi2017mapsy]. The paper's introduction gives the figure as the share of inherited disease alleles that an algorithm expects to mis-splice to some degree. Its citation is a single 2011 study whose last author is also the last author of the assay paper. So the one-third figure is a prediction, restated by people close to it, and not an independent voice in the debate.

### Ascertainment, not biology, orders the estimates

Where estimates can be compared on one variant class, the spread tracks how each set of variants was collected. A de novo mutation burden analysis across 7,833 developmental disorder probands and their parents apportioned 73 per cent of the pathogenic contribution among splice-region variants to the canonical dinucleotides, and 27 per cent to flanking non-canonical positions [@lord2019splicesites]. Published estimates of that non-canonical share disagree by more than twofold. The same paper's discussion sets its own 27 per cent against roughly 30 per cent from HGMD, roughly 43 per cent from another curation and roughly 17 per cent in ClinVar, attributing the spread to ascertainment. ClinVar pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants overlapping the same positions, with non-synonymous consequences removed, put 83.5 per cent in canonical positions and 16.5 per cent in non-canonical ones, or 17.5 per cent after adjusting for submission count. The trio estimate carried a 95 per cent confidence interval of 18 to 39 per cent, so the clinical figures fall below it rather than near it. Those authors read the gap as clinical databases under-ascertaining non-canonical splicing variants by about 35 to 40 per cent. They also call their own 27 per cent a lower bound, since the analysis omitted exonic missense variants, branchpoints, splicing enhancers and silencers, and deep-intronic mutations. They decline, even so, to dismiss the two larger figures on scope grounds, noting that each sits closer to their 27 per cent than to the ClinVar figure.

Part of the disagreement is that the two databases behind these fractions do not classify to one standard. ClinVar takes more than 93 per cent of its content from clinical laboratories, which usually classify by the standardised guidelines, and it lists benign and uncertain variants as well as pathogenic ones [@sharo2023clinvarhgmd]. HGMD is compiled from published papers and keeps no entry for a variant called benign or uncertain, so a fraction computed over HGMD has no benign denominator available to it. The consequence is measurable. Taking inborn errors of metabolism screened in newborns as a test case, where a pathogenic genotype in a healthy adult is almost certainly a classification error, the set HGMD marks disease-causing points to roughly a hundredfold more sick people among 1000 Genomes participants than the set ClinVar marks pathogenic or likely pathogenic.

### Inside and outside the reporter assay's denominator

The assay behind the 10 per cent figure has a narrower denominator than "exonic disease alleles" suggests. Its 4,964 variants were non-synonymous mutations classified disease-causing in HGMD as accessed in May 2012, restricted to internal exons of 100 nucleotides or fewer that fitted a 170-nucleotide genomic window [@soemedi2017mapsy]. Synonymous variants are absent from that denominator altogether, so the figure is a rate among non-synonymous variants in short exons. With the average internal human exon around 130 nucleotides, those authors say half of human exons are inaccessible to the assay. Flanking splice sites, differing transcription efficiencies and tissue-specific effects are not preserved in the construct at all.

A later benchmark names its exclusion in similar terms. The saturation splicing assays it used left out deep-intronic variants, because the lengths involved are hard to screen, and those authors call deep-intronic variants an important source of pathogenic splice disruption [@smith2023splicebenchmark]. Plasmid-borne assays also cannot register effects that come from transcription elongation rate or nucleosome positioning. The same passage then defends the assay. It is generally well correlated with patient material, has a sufficient track record to be accepted as functional evidence, and may still flag a variant correctly even where it gets the outcome wrong. Both halves of that have support. Of 32 exonic splicing mutations the massively parallel assay had called, 26, about 81 per cent, were confirmed in RNA from patient lymphoblastoid cell lines, fibroblasts, whole blood or post-mortem brain [@soemedi2017mapsy]. Those samples were chosen solely on availability, and some confirmations came from published follow-up studies rather than fresh work. An audit of construct-based datasets found complete or high concordance in the predominant transcript between minigene and patient-derived RNA for 81 of 91 variants, 89 per cent, in seven named cancer-predisposition genes [@canson2026constructassays].

So the denominators do not overlap. Non-synonymous variants in short exons, saturation assays with deep-intronic variants excluded, and a cohort in which only deep-intronic diagnoses were counted are three different populations. They cannot be added, and they cannot be set against each other as like for like.

### Creating a splice site against destroying one

Creating a splice site and destroying one are usually counted together, and they behave differently. Of 24,445 variants in the extended acceptor region reported to ClinVar and the Leiden Open Variation Database across 2,100 genes, 638 pathogenic variants lay outside the essential AG [@bryen2022agexclusion]. Within that subset creation was the largest single category: 41.9 per cent created a new AG dinucleotide in the polypyrimidine tract, against 28.2 per cent that removed the pyrimidine at position minus 3 and 16.6 per cent that removed one or more pyrimidines. Those are shares of 638 pathogenic acceptor-region variants, not of pathogenic splicing variants generally, and the donor side is not covered at all. The three mechanisms therefore rank by frequency among pathogenic variants, which is not a ranking of risk per variant.

What risk per variant looks like is visible for the creation class alone. Of 1,077 AG-creating variants in the acceptor region, 262 were classified pathogenic and 198 benign, with 496 uncertain and 121 conflicting. Position separates them. Of the pathogenic ones 93.1 per cent lay in a window more than 6 nucleotides downstream of the predicted branchpoint and more than 5 upstream of the acceptor, against 44.9 per cent of the benign ones. Naturally occurring AG dinucleotides are correspondingly depleted from that window, with only 5.8 per cent of them falling in it.

### Synonymous, intronic and modifier alleles

The accounting is hardest for variants that look harmless in the protein. A de novo synonymous MECP2 variant, c.48C>T, in a girl with Rett syndrome creates a splice donor inside exon 1 that removes the last 16 nucleotides of that exon [@sheikh2013mecp2synonymous]. Translation of the E1 isoform starts in exon 1, while in E2 the same stretch lies in the 5' untranslated region, so the deletion frameshifts only E1. A 5' nuclease assay on patient lymphocyte RNA found the correct E1 transcript reduced about twofold and E2 raised about 1.4-fold against three healthy controls. Elsewhere a splicing effect sits alongside an amino-acid change rather than replacing it. Of 18 synonymous or missense ABCA4 variants drawn from 5,579 biallelic Stargardt probands and tested in midigenes carrying 4 to 12 kilobases of genomic sequence, 16 produced splice aberrations [@kaltak2023abca4splicing]. Fifteen of the 18 were picked because SpliceAI predicted a new acceptor or donor above a delta score of 0.20, so the set was enriched by design.

Modifier alleles turn the fraction into a gradient. In nasal brushings from 13 subjects homozygous for one CFTR intron 9 (TG)mTn haplotype, the proportion of CFTR mRNA retaining exon 10 fell stepwise with tract composition, from 94.7 per cent for (TG)10T9 to 27.6 per cent for (TG)12T5 [@sterrantino2021cftrtgtn]. Two of those five haplotype points rest on one and two subjects. In 548,300 individuals sequenced at one clinical laboratory, the CFTR T5 allele had a minor allele frequency of 4.2 per cent [@nykamp2020polyt]. Paired with a cystic fibrosis-causing variant, its three haplotypes were enriched to graded degrees, with odds ratios of 6.9 for TG11T5, 32.0 for TG12T5 and 78.8 for TG13T5.

### Deep-intronic variants and pseudoexons

Deep-intronic variants are the clearest case of a class sitting outside the denominators above. Short-read genome sequencing of 353 families, drawn from one research study of suspected Mendelian conditions with prior inconclusive clinical testing plus two cardiac cohorts, produced a molecular diagnosis in 54 families, 15.3 per cent of those sequenced [@pitsava2024pseudoexons]. Five of those 54 diagnoses were deep-intronic variants, every one shown by RNA sequencing to create a pseudoexon, and every one with an inconclusive SpliceAI score. Five of 54 solved families and 5 of 353 sequenced families are both defensible fractions, and they are not the same statistic.

Ascertainment shapes even a single well-studied allele. An international cohort for the COL6A1 intron 11 variant reached 44 patients, with its ascertainment broken down explicitly [@foley2024col6a1intron11]. Four were found by muscle RNA sequencing in patients negative for a causative variant in COL6A1, COL6A2 and COL6A3, and 27 by targeted Sanger sequencing of that intron in further such patients. The remaining 13 appeared only after the variant was added to diagnostic sequencing panels. The mechanism is that c.930+189C>T creates a splice donor which, with a cryptic acceptor upstream of it, inserts an in-frame 72-nucleotide pseudoexon into the mature transcript [@bolduc2024col6a1mouse]. Thirteen of those 44 patients were invisible until the variant was looked for by name.
